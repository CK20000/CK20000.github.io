Flapping-Wing Robot — Full-Stack Design, Simulation & Prototype
2023 – 2024 · Semester 5 Mechatronics Project · University of Moratuwa · GitHub ↗
wingspan:110 cm root chord:20 cm weight:4.3 N gearbox:1:14 (3-stage) motor:1000 kV @ 7.4 V optimal flap:6 Hz

WHAT IT DOES

A complete bio-inspired ornithopter — designed, simulated, fabricated, and force-validated end-to-end. From a Solid Edge assembly through multibody dynamics and CFD through a custom ROS+Gazebo simulator through a physical prototype flying on an ESP32-controlled radio link.

110 cm wingspan, 4.3 N final weight, single-wing mechanism with a 1:14 multi-stage gearbox, servo-actuated tail for roll/pitch/yaw authority, and a three-load-cell test bench that closed the loop between numerical prediction and physical measurement.

WHY IT MATTERS TO ME

This was my turning point. It was the first time I carried a system from CAD through FEA, CFD, multibody simulation, embedded communication, and physical prototyping — and the first time I discovered that the failure modes of a simulation are just as instructive as the failure modes of hardware. Everything I've built since traces back to problems I first encountered here.

INITIAL SIZING — FROM BIOLOGY TO NUMBER

Wing geometry was derived from avian scaling laws rather than arbitrary choice. Aspect ratio and target flapping frequency were computed from first principles before any CAD: 

// Aspect ratio from span and planform area
AR = span² / area

// Required flapping frequency (empirical ornithopter scaling law)
f_required = (1.08 / span) · (m²g² / (ρ² · area))^(1/4)

// Motor frequency given gearbox reduction and supply voltage
f_motor = (MR / GR) · V

An elliptical wing planform was selected after comparing loading characteristics against five biological wing types (flycatcher, swallow, albatross, hawk, generic soaring). The elliptical profile minimizes induced drag at the root, which matters most during the high-lift low-speed phase of each flap cycle.

WING MECHANISM — WHY SINGLE-WING, NOT FOLDING

Three candidate mechanisms were modeled: folding wing with 4-bar linkage (biomimetic but complex), and single-piece wing (simple, robust). The single-wing structure was chosen to reduce part count, minimize fuselage integration complexity, and keep the wing structure light enough to hit the target flap frequency without exciting its first vibration mode.

Trade-off honestly stated: this sacrifices the folding mechanics of avian flight, which limits peak efficiency at high speeds. For a first-generation prototype, reliability won over theoretical performance.

GEARBOX — 1:14 MULTI-STAGE

Three-stage reduction (pinion → intermediate → output) was designed in Solid Edge's gear module with a target reduction of 1:14. This provides the torque multiplication needed to drive the wing linkage against aerodynamic damping while keeping the motor in its efficient RPM band.

  • Structural mass reduction achieved through wall thinning and topology-optimized output gear
  • Final base frame: 3 mm thick, 45 mm long, printed in PLA
  • Coupler links redesigned twice — first version failed cyclic fatigue under flapping loads

STRUCTURAL VALIDATION (MSC ADAMS)

Multibody dynamics simulations predicted torque and force on every joint and bracket across a full flapping stroke. Results drove material and geometry iteration:

  • Ball joints: peak force magnitudes reached ±0.15 N — well within printed joint capacity after redesign
  • Brackets: peak torque predictions guided ribbed geometries that eliminated the first failure mode (delamination at layer lines)
  • Output gear: topology optimization removed ~30% of mass while keeping stress below the PLA yield derated for FDM anisotropy

AERODYNAMIC ANALYSIS

Two independent methods cross-validated each other:

  • Vortex Lattice Method — first-pass estimate of lift, drag, and thrust curves across the flapping cycle. Converged for low frequencies but broke down at high flapping rates (see Post-Mortem).
  • ANSYS 2D CFD — parameterized by chord length, angle of attack, and relative velocity; cross-sections sampled at multiple spanwise locations. Used as the ground truth where VLM failed.
  • ANSYS 3D CFD on the full wing was attempted but deemed computationally impractical for the iterative loop — 2D results were within the VLM envelope and adopted.

TAIL CONTROL AUTHORITY

ANSYS simulations of the servo-actuated tail at multiple positions quantified yaw, pitch, and roll moments as a function of flight velocity. Key result: single tail rotation produces substantial roll authority with minor yaw; double rotation combinations provide the independent pitch/yaw mixing needed for coordinated turns.

WHY ROS+GAZEBO INSTEAD OF MATLAB/SIMULINK

The convention in flapping-wing literature is to simulate in MATLAB/Simulink. That is a closed-loop workflow tied to a proprietary toolchain, and it produces simulations that are difficult to integrate with real robot middleware (ROS), cannot be easily shared with the wider robotics community, and are usually re-implemented from scratch by every researcher.

This project built the aerodynamic model directly in ROS + Gazebo, so the simulation lives in the same middleware ecosystem as modern UAV stacks. The ROS package is published open-source as an alternative path for the robotics community.

CUSTOM AERODYNAMIC PLUGINS

Four plugins apply forces directly to the flapping model at each simulation step: 

flapping_controller_plugin     // Wing motion driven by motor velocity
pitching_controller_plugin     // Tail pitch → pitching moment
rolling_controller_plugin      // Asymmetric flapping → roll moment
lift_drag_plugin               // Aerodynamic forces per flap phase

DATA PATH — FROM ADAMS TO GAZEBO

The aerodynamic forces applied inside Gazebo are not invented. They come from a chain of simulations:

  1. MSC Adams predicts wing center-of-mass velocities through the flapping stroke
  2. Those velocities feed the Vortex Lattice Method to compute induced velocities on each wing panel
  3. ANSYS 2D CFD provides ground-truth lift and drag coefficients at representative cross-sections
  4. Resulting force curves are sampled per flap phase and applied inside Gazebo as alternating forces

The core insight exploited: membrane-wing aeroelasticity produces net positive average lift per flap cycle, unlike a rigid wing which can produce zero net lift at certain frequencies. This is why the flexible wing outperformed the rigid variant on the physical test bench.

FLIGHT SIMULATOR GUI

A custom Python GUI drove the Gazebo simulation with live throttle, pitch, and roll sliders, plus real-time readout of height, acceleration, velocity, and flapping frequency. The same GUI structure was later reused to command the physical transmitter over RF, giving a single operator interface for both simulation and hardware.

EXPERIMENTAL VALIDATION

A three-load-cell test bench (10 kg range each, Arduino-based data acquisition) measured vertical and horizontal force at takeoff. The bench was the ground truth — where numerical prediction and physical measurement disagreed, physical measurement won and drove the next iteration.

FAILURE 1 — VLM CONVERGENCE AT HIGH FLAP FREQUENCIES

The Vortex Lattice Method was the primary aerodynamic solver early in the project. It worked cleanly at 2 Hz, degraded at 4 Hz, and produced divergent results at 6 Hz — the exact frequency the design needed to hit. Root cause: strongly unsteady wake dynamics that the steady-state VLM formulation assumes away.

Fix: Hybrid approach. Retained VLM for low-frequency envelope estimation, switched to ANSYS 2D CFD at representative cross-sections for high-frequency behavior. The 2D CFD results agreed with the VLM envelope where the VLM still converged, giving confidence in the CFD-only regime.

FAILURE 2 — 3D-PRINTED STRUCTURAL FAILURES

FDM-printed PLA has anisotropic strength: parts are strong in the print plane and weak at layer boundaries. Early brackets and coupler links failed under cyclic flapping loads — not from peak stress, but from fatigue along layer lines.

Fix: Three-pronged. Increased wall thickness at high-stress corners, changed print orientation so layer lines ran perpendicular to the load direction, and redesigned the ball joints entirely into a through-bolt geometry that eliminated the failure location.

FAILURE 3 — AUTO-FLIGHT CONTROL DESCOPED

The original plan included full IMU-based semi-autonomous flight with switchable manual/auto modes over an ESP32 + nRF24L01 link. The receiver hardware was fabricated and the schematic designed, but integrating attitude estimation into a reliable radio link within the project timeline proved infeasible.

Decision: Descoped autonomy to keep the prototype flying under manual control. This was the right engineering call — a working manual prototype is worth more than a broken autonomous one — but it left a clear direction for future work.

DESIGN LIMITATION — SINGLE-WING TRADE-OFF

The choice of a single-wing mechanism simplified the design and reduced weight, but it cannot replicate the folding mechanics of avian flight. This limits peak aerodynamic efficiency at high speeds and constrains the range of achievable flight regimes. Documented rather than hidden — future iterations would need a folding mechanism to close this gap.

WHAT THIS TAUGHT ME

Every failure here was a mismatch between the assumptions of a tool and the physics of the real system. VLM assumed steady wakes. FDM assumed isotropic material. Auto-flight assumed a clean sensor pipeline. In each case, the fix was not to force the tool to work — it was to recognize the mismatch and switch approaches.

That mindset is what carried directly into the FOC ESC work (where textbook observers assume inductive loads the real motor doesn't have) and the KiCad digital twin (where simulators assume idealized PCBs the real board doesn't have). The failure modes are always more informative than the successes.
